A major insurance client has entered into a Bermudan payer swaption - a contract that gives them the right to enter a fixed-for-floating interest rate swap at any of several future exercise windows. Unlike its European cousin (exercisable only at a single expiry) or its American counterpart (exercisable continuously), the Bermudan occupies a uniquely challenging middle ground: it demands optimal stopping theory on a lattice, careful calibration to a multi-dimensional volatility surface, and a framework that gracefully handles the interdependence between early-exercise boundaries and discount curve dynamics.

Your task: rebuild the pricing stack from first principles - the same exercise every quant on the street must master:

Key Definitions
Interest Rate Swap: A contract between two parties to exchange interest payments. In a fixed-for-floating swap, one party pays a fixed rate and receives a floating rate (e.g., SOFR) on a notional amount.

European Swaption: An option giving the holder the right to enter into an interest rate swap at a specified future date. It can only be exercised on that single date.

Bermudan Swaption: An option giving the holder the right to enter into an interest rate swap on any one of several specified exercise dates - but only once. More valuable than European, less than American.

Payer Swaption: A swaption where the holder has the right to enter a swap paying the fixed rate and receiving the floating rate. It becomes more valuable when interest rates rise.

Normal (Bachelier) Implied Volatility: The market convention for quoting swaption prices. The volatility parameter (in bps/year) that, when input to the Bachelier pricing formula, reproduces the market price.

Hull-White Model: A one-factor Gaussian short-rate model with mean reversion: dr(t) = [θ(t) − a·r(t)] dt + σ(t) dW(t), where a is the mean reversion speed, σ(t) is the volatility, and θ(t) is chosen to exactly fit the initial yield curve.

Trinomial Tree: A lattice method for numerically solving the short-rate model. At each node, the rate can move up, stay in the middle, or move down. Used for early-exercise pricing.

Monte Carlo / Longstaff-Schwartz: A simulation method that generates random rate paths and uses cross-sectional regression at each exercise date to estimate continuation values. Enables early-exercise pricing without a lattice.

Jamshidian's Decomposition: A technique for pricing coupon bond options (swaptions) under one-factor models. Because all bond prices are monotonic in the single state variable, a swaption decomposes into a portfolio of zero-coupon bond options - each with a closed-form Hull-White solution.

Task 1: Yield Curve Construction (10 points)
Construct discount factors and instantaneous forward rates from the provided zero curve.

Formulas:

Discount factor: P(0,T) = exp(−r(T)·T)
Forward rate: f(0, T*i) = −[ln P(0, T*{i+1}) − ln P(0, T*i)] / (T*{i+1} − T_i) for each maturity except the last. For the last maturity, use the backward difference.
For maturities not on the grid, use linear interpolation on continuously-compounded zero rates.
Task 2: Hull-White Model Calibration (30 points)
Model
dr(t) = [θ(t) − a·r(t)] dt + σ(t) dW(t)

a = mean reversion speed (constant, to be calibrated)
σ(t) = piecewise-constant volatility (to be calibrated)
θ(t) = drift function determined analytically from the yield curve (not free)
Piecewise-Constant Volatility
The σ(t) breakpoints align to the exercise dates and swap end:

Breakpoints: [0, T₁, T₂, ..., Tₖ, swap_end] where T₁...Tₖ are the exercise dates. The number of sigma parameters N equals the number of breakpoint intervals. For example, with exercise_dates = [1, 2, 3, 4] and swap_end = 5:

Parameter	Period
σ₁	t ∈ [0, 1]
σ₂	t ∈ (1, 2]
σ₃	t ∈ (2, 3]
σ₄	t ∈ (3, 4]
σ₅	t ∈ (4, 5]
Important: The number of sigma parameters varies across test cases because exercise dates differ. Your code must determine N dynamically from the input.

Calibration Objective
Minimize the RMSE between model-implied and market normal volatilities (in bps) across all swaption quotes provided in the input.

Suggested starting point: [0.05, 0.008, 0.008, ..., 0.008] (mean reversion followed by N sigma values). You are free to use any optimizer and any other startiing point to minimize rmse.

Task 3: Bermudan Swaption Pricing (30 points)
Price the Bermudan payer swaption using your calibrated Hull-White model.

Method
You may use a recombining trinomial/binomial lattice, Monte Carlo simulation with Longstaff-Schwartz regression, or any other numerical method capable of handling early exercise. The primary objective is to produce prices that are close to the reference values. You can try to benchmark your answers against different methods.

Exercise Logic
This is a co-terminal Bermudan swaption: regardless of which exercise date is chosen, the underlying swap always ends at swap_end. For example with swap_end=5:

Exercising at year 1 enters a 4-year swap (years 1→5)
Exercising at year 2 enters a 3-year swap (years 2→5)
Exercising at year 4 enters a 1-year swap (years 4→5)
Optimal exercise decision:

At each exercise date: V = max(exercise_value, continuation_value)
At non-exercise times: V = discounted expected continuation value
Exercise value: compute the value of entering the swap (paying fixed at strike) given current rates, using the Hull-White analytical bond price formula at tree nodes or simulated rate paths.
Task 4: Delta and Vega Risk (30 points)
Compute bucketed sensitivities using bump-and-reprice:

Vega (sensitivity to each vol quote)
For each of the swaption quotes in swaption_vols:

1. Bump that vol quote by +1 bp

2. Re-calibrate the model to the bumped surface

3. Re-price the Bermudan with the re-calibrated model

4. Vega = bumped_price − base_price (in dollars)

Delta (sensitivity to each zero rate)
For each zero rate point in zero_curve:

1. Bump that zero rate by +1 bp (+0.0001)

2. Re-calibrate the model (same vol surface, bumped curve)

3. Re-price the Bermudan with the re-calibrated model

4. Delta = bumped_price − base_price (in dollars)
